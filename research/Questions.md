# Problem-space discovery questions

These questions map the learning Tech & AI ecosystem for non-technical professionals. They are intended to separate observations from explanatory insights, surface incentives and constraints across every participant, and produce testable problem hypotheses.

## 1. Defining the ecosystem and its boundaries

1. Who are the relevant participants in the Tech & AI learning ecosystem for non-technical professionals?
2. Which participants directly experience the problem, and which influence or pay for its resolution?
3. What does “non-technical” actually mean in this context: job role, prior education, confidence, access, skill level, or something else?
4. Which outcomes matter to each participant: confidence, productivity, employability, promotion, revenue, risk reduction, or innovation?
5. Where does the learning journey begin and end today?
6. Which parts of the journey happen individually, at work, in communities, or through formal education?
7. Which external forces are changing the need for these skills—job expectations, AI adoption, company policy, market competition, or career anxiety?
8. What must be true for someone to say, “I can confidently understand, use, and build with technology and AI”?
9. Are “understand,” “use,” and “build” separate jobs to be done, or sequential stages of one journey?
10. Which of these outcomes are valuable but not realistically necessary for every user?

## 2. Learner segmentation

11. Which non-technical professional segments have the strongest need to become more capable with Tech & AI?
12. How do needs differ across marketers, product managers, HR professionals, founders, finance professionals, operations professionals, and students?
13. Which segment experiences the problem frequently enough to form a sustained learning habit?
14. Who has the highest urgency, willingness to invest time, and willingness to pay?
15. Who has the lowest baseline confidence, and what specifically causes that lack of confidence?
16. Which learners are seeking career mobility versus immediate help in their current job?
17. Which learners already use AI tools casually but cannot apply them reliably at work?
18. Which learners understand concepts but struggle to execute?
19. Which learners can complete tutorials but cannot independently solve a real work problem?
20. How do users differ by work environment: startup, large company, agency, freelancer, or job seeker?
21. What prior experiences cause users to believe that technical learning is “not for people like me”?
22. What constraints do they have around time, money, language, device access, attention, and employer support?
23. Who is likely to abandon learning earliest, and why?
24. Who is already inventing workarounds to learn or use AI?

## 3. Learner problem discovery

25. What exact moment triggers a learner to seek Tech & AI education?
26. What job, task, or career risk are they trying to address at that moment?
27. What do they believe they need to learn before they begin?
28. What do they actually need to learn to make progress on their job?
29. Where do learners get stuck: choosing a topic, understanding concepts, practising, applying, troubleshooting, or sustaining a habit?
30. What makes a learner feel intimidated by technology or AI?
31. Which jargon, concepts, workflows, or interfaces create the greatest friction?
32. What misconceptions prevent learners from starting or progressing?
33. Why does access to abundant courses, videos, and AI tools not lead to capability?
34. What do learners do immediately after consuming a course or tutorial?
35. What prevents learned knowledge from being used in a real work context?
36. How do learners currently know what to learn next?
37. What happens when a learner encounters an error, fails at a task, or receives an unexpected AI output?
38. What forms of support do users seek, from whom, and how quickly do they receive it?
39. What does a successful learning session look like to the learner?
40. What evidence makes a learner feel genuinely more capable rather than temporarily motivated?
41. What causes users to return the next day or week?
42. What causes them to churn?
43. What risks do learners perceive around using AI at work—quality, privacy, accuracy, job security, or embarrassment?
44. What would make a learner trust an AI-led learning experience?

## 4. The employer and manager ecosystem

45. Which employers need their non-technical teams to become more capable with Tech & AI?
46. What business problem are employers trying to solve: productivity, adoption, innovation, cost, quality, retention, or hiring?
47. Which teams and roles are expected to use AI today, and which are only encouraged to experiment?
48. How do managers assess whether their teams have a real capability gap?
49. What do managers consider proof that an employee can use Tech & AI effectively?
50. What are managers’ biggest concerns: time away from work, poor quality outputs, data exposure, compliance, or uneven adoption?
51. What incentives do managers have to support learning, and what prevents them from doing so?
52. How do employer policies, approved tools, data permissions, and budgets affect learning choices?
53. Are learners allowed to apply new skills in their work immediately?
54. What workplace tasks would make the best safe practice environments?
55. How do managers currently communicate skill expectations and give feedback on AI-enabled work?
56. What happens when an employee’s AI use creates a mistake or risk?
57. Who owns AI capability development internally: L&D, HR, IT, functional leaders, or employees themselves?
58. Would employers pay for a platform, and what outcome would justify that spend?

## 5. Learning providers and creators

59. Who currently teaches this audience: universities, bootcamps, cohort programs, course platforms, YouTube creators, consultants, and employers?
60. What incentives shape the content these providers create?
61. How do providers select topics, learning formats, and difficulty levels?
62. What learner outcomes do providers currently measure?
63. What prevents providers from teaching applied, role-specific capability rather than generic concepts?
64. How do creators keep content current as AI tools and workflows change?
65. What does the provider need in order to offer interactive practice, feedback, and personalization?
66. Where do existing providers lose learners?
67. Which providers have already built strong trust with the target learners?
68. Could a new product complement them rather than compete with them?
69. What integration, distribution, or content partnership incentives could exist?

## 6. Tools, platforms, and technical ecosystem

70. Which AI tools, automation platforms, no-code tools, data tools, APIs, and collaboration tools are relevant to the target user’s work?
71. Which tools are already available to the learner versus blocked by cost, access, or company policy?
72. What foundational concepts must users understand to use these tools safely and effectively?
73. Which skills are durable despite fast-changing tools, and which are tool-specific?
74. What kinds of tasks can a beginner complete using existing tools without coding?
75. Where do tools themselves create friction through complex setup, pricing, terminology, integrations, or errors?
76. What technical concepts are essential for confidence but can be taught without turning learners into engineers?
77. What does “build with AI” mean for each user segment: prompts, workflows, automations, agents, dashboards, prototypes, or API-enabled products?
78. What safeguards are needed when learners practise with real data, third-party tools, or automation?
79. How can a learning platform verify that a learner completed a real task rather than merely watched content?
80. Which tool vendors already provide education, templates, certifications, or onboarding for this audience?

## 7. Current learner journeys and communication flows

81. How does a learner currently move from curiosity to selecting a resource?
82. Where do they discover courses, tools, communities, and advice?
83. Who influences their choice: peers, managers, creators, social media, employer, or algorithmic recommendations?
84. How do learners communicate their needs to managers, mentors, instructors, or peers?
85. How do managers communicate required skills and safe-use rules to employees?
86. How do instructors or platforms collect learner feedback and detect confusion?
87. Where is context lost between learning content and a learner’s real work?
88. How do learners share what they built, ask for help, and receive feedback?
89. How are success stories, templates, and proven workflows circulated within teams or communities?
90. What handoffs exist between content consumption, practice, feedback, and workplace application?
91. Which communication channels are most important—WhatsApp, Slack, LinkedIn, email, workplace tools, live cohorts, or in-product support?
92. Where do these current channels fail: speed, quality, relevance, trust, or accessibility?

## 8. Existing alternatives and unmet needs

93. What alternatives do learners use today: courses, YouTube, AI chatbots, mentors, peers, experimentation, or formal programs?
94. What job does each alternative perform well?
95. What does each alternative fail to solve?
96. Which existing products have solved motivation, structure, practice, personalization, accountability, or application particularly well?
97. Which Duolingo-like mechanisms have been tried in professional or technical learning, and for whom?
98. Why do current learning platforms often produce completion without capability?
99. Why do AI assistants not fully solve learning-by-doing on their own?
100. What workarounds do users create when existing options fail?
101. What do users pay for today, and what are they unwilling to pay for?
102. What would make them switch from their current solution?
103. Is there an underserved intersection of role, task, skill level, and context that existing players overlook?
104. Which pains are already well solved and therefore should not be our initial focus?

## 9. Learning experience and behavior

105. What learning format best fits users’ real constraints: short lessons, guided projects, live cohorts, simulations, workplace challenges, or peer learning?
106. What is the smallest meaningful practice activity that creates a real feeling of progress?
107. What progression should a learner follow from awareness to independent application?
108. When should the platform teach concepts, and when should it let learners learn through doing?
109. What kind of feedback helps a non-technical learner recover from mistakes without losing confidence?
110. What type of personalization is useful: role, experience level, current task, goal, available tools, or employer policy?
111. What would make the experience habit-forming without creating shallow engagement?
112. Which incentives work: visible progress, streaks, portfolio projects, peer recognition, workplace outcomes, credentials, or career opportunities?
113. How can a product prevent gamification from becoming a substitute for capability?
114. What support needs to be AI-led, peer-led, expert-led, or manager-led?
115. What edge cases emerge when a learner has no clear goal, lacks workplace access, progresses unusually fast, or is overwhelmed?
116. How should the product respond when a learner’s task falls outside its curriculum?
117. What accessibility, language, and cultural needs affect the experience?

## 10. Outcomes, proof, and measurement

118. What is the learner’s desired outcome in measurable behavioral terms?
119. What is the employer’s desired outcome in measurable business terms?
120. What leading behaviors indicate that a learner is becoming capable?
121. What evidence distinguishes confidence from actual competence?
122. What is the first meaningful activation event for each learner segment?
123. What repeat behavior would demonstrate retained value?
124. What real-world work outcome can be attributed, at least directionally, to the learning experience?
125. How can we measure successful application without accessing sensitive work data?
126. What signals show a learner is stuck, disengaged, or at risk of churn?
127. Which metrics could be gamed and therefore should not be treated as success?
128. What would make the platform valuable enough for learners to recommend it?
129. What would make an employer renew or expand usage?
130. What outcomes must be measured before claiming that the product improves capability?

## 11. Testable problem hypotheses

131. Which user segment faces the highest-cost capability gap?
132. What is the underlying reason that this gap persists despite existing learning resources?
133. What evidence would disprove that problem hypothesis?
134. Which pain occurs frequently, causes meaningful consequences, and remains poorly served?
135. Which ecosystem participant has both motivation and ability to change the learner’s outcome?
136. What minimal intervention could test whether solving this pain changes learner behavior?
137. What assumptions are we currently making about user motivation, willingness to practise, tool access, employer support, and ability to pay?
138. Which assumption is riskiest to the viability of the product?
139. What should we learn first before designing the MVP?
140. How might our own familiarity with technology be biasing how we define the learner’s problem?

## 12. Additional discovery angles


141. Which compliance, legal, IT-security, or procurement gatekeepers must approve tool or data use before an employee can practise with real work data, and what specifically do they screen for?
142. Whose budget actually funds the solution — the learner's own money, a team L&D budget, or manager discretion — and what proof or threshold triggers a yes to pay?
143. Which specific named products or programs have already attempted Duolingo-style mechanics for professional or technical learning, and what specific mechanism caused each to fail, plateau, or succeed?
144. Why hasn't a well-resourced, obviously-incentivized player (Duolingo, Coursera, LinkedIn Learning, an AI tools vendor) already solved this problem — and what does that imply about whether the problem is real, correctly scoped, or solvable at all?
145. For each major observation collected, has "why" been chained at least three levels deep before it is treated as a validated insight — and what is the underlying root cause reached at that third level?
146. Is the interview and research sample diverse across network, geography, industry, and seniority, or drawn primarily from the researcher's own circle — and what would change about the findings if it weren't?
147. Does the problem — "non-technical professional struggling with AI/tech" — look the same across different geographies and markets (e.g., urban India vs. US vs. LatAm), given differences in baseline tech access, language fluency, and employer AI-adoption pressure? Which geography is this case study targeting, and why?
148. Beyond the learner and employer, which stakeholders validate or benefit from the learner's demonstrated capability rather than the capability itself — recruiters, hiring managers, credentialing bodies, investors — and is "signaling" capability in scope for this product or explicitly excluded?
