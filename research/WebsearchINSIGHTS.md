# Web-search insights: non-technical-professional Tech & AI learning

Research date: 16 August 2026. This document synthesizes the source register in [WebsearchsecondaryresearchSOURCES.md](WebsearchsecondaryresearchSOURCES.md). It contains 14 causal insights, not observations or solution proposals.

An evidentiary tag applies to the insight exactly as written. It does not indicate how important the problem might be.

## Learner behavior and learning transfer

### Insight 1: The learning market can grow while workplace capability remains scarce because content consumption and skill transfer are governed by different systems.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: courses and AI-learning resources are widely available. Level 2: completing or viewing them does not require users to perform a representative work task. Level 3: transfer depends on learner readiness, practice design, feedback, and work-environment support—conditions a content library does not control. This explains why more content does not automatically create more applied capability.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [Shukla (2024) systematic review of online-training transfer](https://doi.org/10.1002/hrdq.21518), [Blume et al. transfer meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880), and [Ford, Yelon & Billington](https://doi.org/10.1002/piq.20108) all distinguish learning activity from transfer; [Class Central’s market inventory](https://www.classcentral.com/report/most-popular-courses-2025/) documents the supply abundance.

**What would validate or break this:** Interviews and task walkthroughs must show that target users have completed or consumed existing learning but still cannot independently complete a relevant work task. It breaks if current resources reliably produce independent task performance for the target segment.

### Insight 2: Adult learners’ inconsistency is often a workload-allocation problem, not a motivation problem.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: adults cite time, cost, and unsuitable training as barriers. Level 2: learning competes with work and family obligations. Level 3: the individual is expected to bear the time and economic cost even though employers capture much of the productivity benefit, creating a structural incentive and capacity mismatch.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [OECD Trends in Adult Learning: participation barriers](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/trends-in-adult-learning_ec0624a6-en/full-report/why-is-participation-not-more-common_73893b53.html), [OECD executive summary](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/trends-in-adult-learning_ec0624a6-en/full-report/executive-summary_6b403d28.html), and [OECD participation reforms](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/increasing-adult-learning-participation_cf5d9c21-en/full-report/component-5.html).

**What would validate or break this:** Ask users about a recent abandoned learning attempt: competing work, time, cost, and perceived relevance should explain abandonment more often than simple lack of interest. It breaks if motivated users with protected time still abandon for an unrelated reason.

### Insight 3: “Non-technical” is a weak segmentation variable because task risk—not technical identity—determines the capability a user needs.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: most workers do not need advanced AI engineering skills. Level 2: they need different combinations of data literacy, tool use, evaluation, and judgment. Level 3: those combinations are set by the task, the data involved, and consequences of error, so a broad identity label hides the actual learning job.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [OECD AI and skills](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/ai-and-skills_f843b352-en/full-report.html), [DigComp 2.2](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/oldpage-digcomp/digcomp-framework_en), and the [European Commission’s AI-literacy guidance](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers).

**What would validate or break this:** Compare users within the same job title and across titles. It is supported if task type, risk, and access predict needs better than self-described technical confidence; it breaks if one role has a uniform, stable learning need regardless of task context.

### Insight 4: AI learning creates confidence only when learners can judge output, because AI lowers production effort without lowering the cost of a bad decision.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: AI can produce plausible output quickly. Level 2: a beginner may not know whether the output is accurate, appropriate, safe, or complete. Level 3: responsibility for verification remains with the human user, so using AI without evaluative judgment can increase the risk of confidently wrong action.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [NIST AI RMF FAQs](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs), [NIST Generative AI Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf), and [UNESCO’s GenAI guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=83250).

**What would validate or break this:** Observe users evaluating a real AI output and ask what evidence they use. It breaks if the chosen task has reliable automatic verification and users do not need human judgment to act safely.

### Insight 5: Feedback improves learning only when it changes the learner’s next action; feedback that merely reassures or supplies an answer does not establish capability.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: timely feedback and retrieval practice are associated with better persistence and retention. Level 2: feedback identifies the gap between an attempt and a successful outcome. Level 3: the learning mechanism is the learner’s corrected re-attempt and later independent reuse, not the presence of feedback itself.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [ACM Learning@Scale feedback experiment](https://doi.org/10.1145/3430895.3460991), [ERIC spaced-retrieval study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1290236.pdf), and [EEF metacognition review](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/evidence-reviews/metacognition-and-self-regulation).

**What would validate or break this:** Test whether feedback recipients make a better second attempt and later complete a similar task without help. It breaks if feedback raises satisfaction or completion but not independent task performance.

### Insight 6: AI career anxiety produces top-of-funnel attention, but not necessarily a problem worth paying to solve.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: workers report that AI affects career competitiveness and some express displacement concern. Level 2: anxiety directs people toward content, credentials, and experimentation. Level 3: anxiety does not specify a recurring work task, access to tools, or a measurable outcome, so it can create browsing behavior without creating sustained learning or purchase behavior.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-suggested, single-source** — [Anthropic’s 81,000-user work/economics survey](https://www.anthropic.com/research/81k-economics) reports concern and productivity perceptions but is explicitly non-representative; [Microsoft/LinkedIn’s Work Trend Index](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part/) supports broad career relevance but is vendor-produced.

**What would validate or break this:** Compare users entering from career-anxiety messaging with users entering from a recent work task. It is supported if the first group has lower task specificity, activation, and retention; it breaks if career anxiety reliably translates into recurring applied use and willingness to pay.

## Employer and compliance constraints

### Insight 7: Managers are a causal part of skill transfer because they control whether learning receives a work opportunity.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: supervisor support is associated with stronger training transfer. Level 2: managers allocate time, approve changed workflows, and can provide a task on which to practise. Level 3: without that authority being exercised, learning remains detached from the setting where it is supposed to create value.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [Ghosh et al. on transfer design and supervisor support](https://doi.org/10.1108/JWL-08-2016-0079), [Blume et al. transfer meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880), and [OECD adult-learning summary](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/trends-in-adult-learning_ec0624a6-en/full-report/executive-summary_6b403d28.html).

**What would validate or break this:** Interview managers and learners about a recent learning-to-work attempt. It is supported if time, task assignment, review, or permission from a manager determines whether application occurs; it breaks if users can apply independently without managerial involvement.

### Insight 8: Workplace policy blocks application when it is communicated as a prohibition rather than as task-level decision guidance.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: organizations must manage privacy, security, IP, bias, and accountability risks. Level 2: broad restrictions reduce users’ certainty about what is permitted. Level 3: when policy is not translated into concrete boundaries for actual tasks and data, users either avoid legitimate experimentation or seek informal workarounds; the root gap is operational governance communication.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [NIST Generative AI Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf), [NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), and [European Commission AI-literacy guidance](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers).

**What would validate or break this:** Collect real policies and ask employees to classify realistic tasks as allowed, prohibited, or uncertain. It breaks if policies already give task-level clarity and users can consistently act on them.

### Insight 9: A generic AI curriculum cannot satisfy employer needs because AI literacy is context-dependent by design.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: AI-literacy guidance asks organizations to account for staff knowledge, experience, training, and system context. Level 2: the consequences and oversight required vary by use case. Level 3: one universal course cannot create the judgment needed across different systems and affected people, because the definition of safe competence is embedded in the work context.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [European Commission AI-literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers), [EU AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/faq/how-can-companies-ensure-ai-competency-eg-employee-training), and [NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs).

**What would validate or break this:** Compare the competence required for two distinct target workflows. It breaks only if the workflows have the same tools, data sensitivity, error cost, and oversight requirements.

### Insight 10: Employer ROI remains hard to prove because the buyer’s desired outcome occurs downstream of the metrics most learning products collect.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: employers care about adoption, productivity, quality, and risk, while learning systems commonly record enrolment, activity, and completion. Level 2: those activity measures do not establish transfer. Level 3: the provider and buyer are optimizing against different evidence, creating a measurement gap that prevents a credible causal link between learning spend and operating value.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [WEF Future of Jobs 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/), [Ford, Yelon & Billington](https://doi.org/10.1002/piq.20108), [Blume et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880), and [LinkedIn’s organization-learning flow](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a700799).

**What would validate or break this:** Ask budget holders for the exact evidence required to renew learning spend, then test whether task artifacts and outcome measures change that decision. It breaks if employers buy and renew solely on completion or satisfaction data.

## Competitive landscape and ecosystem handoffs

### Insight 12: The broad AI-learning category is unlikely to be empty because incumbents already own most visible learning components.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: major providers offer role guides, pathways, practice, coaching, no-code content, and credentials. Level 2: these solve selection and basic learning for many users. Level 3: a new entrant cannot assume that adding one familiar component solves an unmet need; the unresolved problem must lie in a handoff that existing nodes do not have the incentive or context to own.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-suggested, single-source** — [LinkedIn Learning’s AI pathways](https://business.linkedin.com/learn/resources/upskilling-and-reskilling/ai-skill-pathways), [Role Guides](https://www.linkedin.com/help/learning/answer/a1429531), and [product updates](https://business.linkedin.com/learn/customer-success-center/product-updates/all-updates/q1-26) establish existing offerings but are vendor sources; [Class Central](https://www.classcentral.com/report/most-popular-courses-2025/) corroborates course abundance, not product effectiveness.

**What would validate or break this:** Conduct switch interviews: users should name an incumbent, describe the moment it stopped helping, and show the workaround. It breaks if target users have not tried alternatives simply because they lack awareness or access.

### Insight 13: The likely ecosystem failure is a loss of context between learning and work, not a lack of information.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: providers know learning behavior, managers know work requirements, and governance teams know constraints. Level 2: those contexts are held in separate systems and conversations. Level 3: because the learner must manually carry role, task, data, and permission context across them, generic instruction loses relevance at the point of application.

**Evidentiary status:** **Assumption — needs primary research** — transfer literature establishes that the work environment matters ([Shukla](https://doi.org/10.1002/hrdq.21518); [Blume et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880)), and NIST establishes context-sensitive governance ([NIST GenAI Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)); neither source directly maps this specific handoff for non-technical professionals.

**What would validate or break this:** Reconstruct a recent learning journey end-to-end and identify where task, tool, data, policy, or feedback context was lost. It breaks if target users already receive this context seamlessly from their employer and learning provider.

### Insight 14: Capability signaling and capability building are distinct jobs, so conflating them can lead to the wrong market.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: credentials and career pathways exist because learners and employers need visible signals. Level 2: a credential can indicate participation or assessment performance without proving workplace application. Level 3: the evaluator’s need to filter candidates and the learner’s need to perform better are different mechanisms, with different buyers, metrics, and alternatives.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-suggested, single-source** — [LinkedIn AI Skill Pathways](https://business.linkedin.com/learn/resources/upskilling-and-reskilling/ai-skill-pathways) documents credentials and skills pathways; [Class Central’s career guidance](https://www.classcentral.com/help/can-moocs-get-job) notes employers vary in how they value course certificates. Neither proves the dominant pain for our segment.

**What would validate or break this:** Ask users to choose between a product that improves next-week work performance and one that produces a recognized signal, with real trade-offs. It breaks if the same evaluator accepts the same task evidence as both performance proof and hiring signal.

## Product-design and research risks

### Insight 15: Global AI adoption evidence cannot select a local product problem because adoption depends on local infrastructure, labor structure, and access conditions.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: AI use and skills vary across countries, industries, and occupations. Level 2: local connectivity, data, language, employer maturity, policies, and task organization shape whether a user can benefit. Level 3: geography is part of the causal environment, so a global trend cannot substitute for a chosen market and a locally grounded user problem.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [World Bank AI Foundations](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/dptr2025-ai-foundations/report), [Anthropic geographic/enterprise adoption research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-geography?type=research), [ILO global jobs update](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update), and the [IMF Philippines study](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/2025/043/article-A000-en.xml).

**What would validate or break this:** Select a geography, then compare local users’ tools, policies, language, internet/device access, and role tasks with the global pattern. It breaks only if these conditions do not materially alter access or task relevance.

### Insight 16: A product can falsely appear successful if it measures learning activity instead of independent, safe application.

**Why this is an insight, not an observation:** Level 1: lessons completed, time spent, certificates, and streaks are easy to record. Level 2: those measures do not require independent use on a representative task. Level 3: when activity is used as the success metric, the product is incentivized to optimize visible engagement rather than the harder downstream outcome of reliable application.

**Evidentiary status:** **Secondary-research-supported** — [Ford, Yelon & Billington](https://doi.org/10.1002/piq.20108), [Blume et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880), and the [Costa Rica online-job-training experiment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387824000348).

**What would validate or break this:** Define a representative independent task and quality/safety rubric before building. It breaks if conventional engagement metrics strongly and consistently predict later independent application for the target segment.
